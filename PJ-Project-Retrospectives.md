# Project Retrospectives

Welcome to the project retrospectives training. In this module, we'll provide the foundational knowledge you need to successfully conduct retros on your projects. This training is geared toward project managers but relevant to all CivicActions employees. It provides the context for us to understand and embrace this initiative. This course will help you:

- Understand how retrospectives work
- Participate meaningfully regardless of role
- Translate insights into meaningful improvements

## What is a Retrospective?

A project retrospective is a structured, collaborative meeting held by a team to reflect on a completed project or work cycle (like a sprint) to analyze successes and failures, with the primary goal of identifying actionable improvements for future work.

At CivicActions, **retrospectives are a core part of how we learn, improve, and collaborate**. We consider them more than just part of agile ceremonies; they're a structured way to:

- Reflect on what worked and what didn’t
- Identify improvements
- Turn insights into action

At their core, retrospectives are about reflection. They give teams space to look back at what they’ve done, identify what worked well, and pinpoint areas for improvement. The ultimate goal is better delivery over time. It's not about perfection, but progress. 

## Why Retrospectives Matter

Across projects, there’s no single way retrospectives are being run—and that’s intentional. Teams use different tools and formats depending on what works best for them. This flexibility keeps retrospectives relevant and useful rather than turning into a checkbox exercise.

Retrospectives are built on a simple idea:

>> **Teams improve when they regularly reflect and adapt**.

--------

Retros help teams:

- Identify what worked well
- Surface challenges and blockers
- Improve collaboration and processes
- Create actionable next steps

They also support:

- Better decision-making
- Stronger team alignment
- Continuous learning and growth

--------

At CivicActions, retrospectives are also a space to:

- Celebrate wins
- Share feedback safely
- Improve without blame

## Who's Involved?

You join your first retrospective session. Who’s in the room?

- Project team members
- Project Manager or Scrum Master
- Product Owner
- Occasionally, observers like internal or external stakeholders, learners, or CORs

While several people and roles can be in the room during the retrospective, that doesn't mean everyone participates. Retros are the time to reflect on the sprint work or project cycle. Therefore, only those who contributed to the work should participate in the retrospective. The focus of the retro isn't to is standardize how it's run, but rather to standardize what comes out of them. That means ensuring key insights and outcomes from those directly involved in the work are consistently documented and accessible.

## Running a Retro

While formats may vary, most retrospectives follow a similar flow. 
- First, you hold the session. 
- Then, you capture insights, including what went well and what didn’t. 
- After that, you summarize key takeaways. 
- And finally, when appropriate, you track action items so improvements don’t get lost.

Teams use a variety of tools to run retrospectives. Some prefer structured platforms like EasyRetro or Parabol, while others use collaborative tools like Mural or even Slack. The tool itself isn’t the priority. It’s what you capture and how you use it afterward. 

Both of those parts are key: **what you capture** and *how you use it*. Retros are most beneficial when you use the information you learn in them to iterate on future work and processes. 

## Types of Retros

Retrospectives can happen at different levels. Let's take a look at a few of the most common retros:

- Sprint retros (biweekly)
- Stakeholder retros (with clients)
- Project retros (periodic reflection)

Sprint retros are typically frequent, often every sprint or every two weeks. Stakeholder retros bring in client perspectives. And project retros take a step back to look at the bigger picture over time. 

Regardless of format or tool, there are a few baseline expectations. Every project should:  

- Run retrospectives regularly, 
- Capture what’s learned, and 
- Track opportunities for improvement as they arise. 

This ensures retrospectives actually lead to change.

## Making Retrospectives Effective

During your next retrospective, the facilitator asks:

- What went well?
- What didn’t go well?
- What should we change?

These are common retrospective prompts.

Other formats include:

- Start / Stop / Continue
- Sad / Mad / Glad
- 4Ls (Loved, Learned, Lacked, Longed)

## From Insights to Continuous Improvement

When retrospectives are done well, they prevent teams from repeating the same mistakes. They also make it easier to share knowledge across projects. Over time, this builds a culture where continuous improvement is part of how we work.

After the retrospective:

- Action items are documented
- Owners are assigned
- Progress is tracked across sprints

## Summary

At CivicActions, improvement isn't accidental; it's intentional, structured, and shared. Retrospectives help drive and iterate improvement because:
